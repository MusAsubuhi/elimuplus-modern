Why we exist

Elimu+ began with a simple observation. Training programmes in our community were leaving too early. People finished a course, received a certificate, and went back to exactly the same conditions, because nobody stayed to help them through the first 90 days of actually running a business.

So Musa Asubuhi and James Kimani set out to build something different: a programme that does not stop at the door. We train women and youth through the churches, NGOs, schools and colleges that already serve them, and then we follow participants into their businesses.

What we actually do

Every participant works through the SEE Canvas (Solutions for Entrepreneurship and Employment): twelve sections, two pages, one business made clear. It is built on our SAME principle, which means Simple, Actionable, Memorable and Experiential, and it is taught in Swahili and English.

Then comes the part that makes the difference. We visit, call and coach at 30, 60 and 90 days, tracking whether each business is still standing and what it needs next. Our north star is a 75% business survival rate at 90 days.

What we have seen so far

From our first adult cohort of ten women at Deliverance Church International, Likoni, at the 60-day follow-up:

8 of 10
businesses confirmed operating (9 of 10 at 30 days)
5 of 10
women reporting rising income, with 4 businesses clearly growing
KES 213,190
invested across the ten businesses

We want to be straight with you: with ten businesses these are early signals, not proof. Figures are reported by participants and checked by our field team where possible. Since 2025 we have trained about 45 young people and adults across four cohorts, and the 90-day visit for our first adult cohort is next.

Every lesson is grounded in a real place

We do not teach with invented examples. Every lesson is built around Mwanahamisi, an omena seller in Soweto estate, Likoni. When participants learn contribution margin, they work it out for her stall. When they map competition, they map hers first. Mwanahamisi sells omena in Likoni, not tomatoes in a case study from Nairobi, and that is the point.
