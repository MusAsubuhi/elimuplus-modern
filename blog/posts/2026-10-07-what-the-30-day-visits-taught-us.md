A month after the training at KE358 Deliverance Church Likoni CDC, we went back to see what had happened to the ten businesses started by that cohort. This is the part of Elimu+ that no certificate can stand in for. It is also the part where we learn the most.

Nine of the ten businesses were still trading, and six of the ten participants told us their weekly income was rising. A seventh was earning about the same but had widened what she sold, moving from cakes and eggs into sausages. Those are good numbers, and we are proud of them, but with ten participants they are a first signal and not a proof. What follows is more useful than the headline.

## Stock runs out before customers do

Seven of the ten said they did not have enough stock. The pattern was almost always the same: customers were coming, the first crate or bale sold well, and then the next purchase was hard to fund. A woman selling vegetables told us the fast movers, such as onions and potatoes, were exactly what she could not afford to restock.

This changes how we think about the canvas session on break-even and daily sales targets. Knowing what you need to sell is only half of it. The other half is knowing what you need to *buy* to keep selling, and when. We are adding a restocking question to the follow-up checklist so we see it at day 30 instead of hearing about it at day 60.

## The kitchen and the till share a pocket

Four of the ten named the same risk: using business capital for home needs. None of them spoke about it as carelessness. A child needed care, a bill came due, and the stock money was the only money in reach. For a small trader, the line between household and business is not a habit problem; it is a cash problem.

We do not think a lecture fixes this. What seems to help is making the leak visible. A participant who writes down what she took out and why can see, in two weeks, that the business is paying her a wage she never agreed to. We will test a simple take-home column in the canvas for the next cohort.

## One business had already stopped

One participant's business was not trading by the time we arrived. She had run out of capital for a second batch after the first did not turn out well, and the packaging she needed was not what she had asked for. She also has a very young baby. Her story is the reason the follow-up exists. If we had waited for the 90-day review, we would have lost three months in which she could have been supported.

## Small fixes that came up in the visits

Not every need was money. One participant needed a food-handling licence from the municipal council before she could sell cakes more widely. Another was losing customers because her stall did not look different from the one beside it, and the practical suggestions were simple: put products outside, use a creative stand, repaint. A third wanted a freezer so she could buy in bulk. These are the conversations that only happen when someone stands in the stall and looks around.

## What we are changing

- Add a restocking plan to the canvas and to the day-30 checklist.
- Pilot a simple "money taken out for home" tally for participants who share their wallet with the business.
- Send partners a short list of small enablers, such as licences and display, that a visit can unlock.
- Keep reporting results as counts as well as percentages, so nobody mistakes ten businesses for ten thousand.

We will keep writing these notes as we go, including the ones where things did not work. If your church, NGO or college would like to see how this follow-through works, we would be glad to talk.
